    Wheat & Barley Q&A: Yemen & Southern Saudi Arabia | أسئلة وأجوبة القمح والشعير: اليمن وجنوب السعودية¶

    Q1: What are the main wheat varieties grown in the Yemen highlands (Dhamar, Sana'a) and the southern Saudi regions (Asir, Najran)?¶

    EN: The Yemen highlands (1,800–2,800 m elevation) and the southern Saudi highlands share similar agro-climatic conditions that support rain-fed and supplemental-irrigated wheat. The principal varieties include:

    Variety Region Type Maturity Key Traits
    Buhuth 22 (بحوث 22) Dhamar, Sana'a plateaus Bread wheat 140–155 days Drought-tolerant, moderate yield (1.8–2.5 t/ha), adapted to terraced fields
    Sana'ani Local (صنعاني محلي) Sana'a basin, Amran Landrace bread wheat 150–170 days Long tradition, low input requirement, straw valued for livestock
    Dhamari (ذماري) Dhamar plateau Landrace 145–160 days Cold-tolerant at altitude, resistance to yellow rust
    Yecora Rojo Asir highlands, Najran Semi-dwarf bread wheat 120–135 days High yield potential (3.0–4.5 t/ha) under irrigation, widely adopted in Saudi programs
    Giza 168 Introduced trials, Dhamar/Ibb Bread wheat 130–145 days Rust-resistant, good grain quality, tested by AREA (Agricultural Research and Extension Authority)
    Hail 1 / Najran Local Najran valley, Asir foothills Bread wheat 125–140 days Heat-tolerant, suited to wadi-edge irrigation

    In Yemen, wheat is planted October–November and harvested April–May on the highland plateaus. Yemeni landraces are prized for their adaptation to marginal rainfall (250–450 mm) and poor soils, but yields remain low (0.8–1.5 t/ha national average) compared to Saudi irrigated wheat (4.0–6.0 t/ha). Saudi Arabia's southern regions benefit from government extension services and subsidized inputs, enabling adoption of improved varieties, while Yemeni farmers rely more heavily on saved seed from local landraces.

    Q2: How is barley cultivated in arid and saline areas of Yemen and Najran, and what varieties are most suitable?¶

    EN: Barley (Hordeum vulgare) is the cereal of last resort in the driest and most saline environments of Yemen and southern Saudi Arabia. It thrives where wheat fails — below 300 mm annual rainfall, in saline soils (EC up to 8–12 dS/m), and on marginal slopes. Key cultivation details:

    Parameter Yemen Arid Zones (Marib, Al-Jawf, Eastern Plateaus) Najran / Southern Saudi
    Rainfall 100–250 mm 80–200 mm + supplemental irrigation
    Soil salinity tolerance Up to 10 dS/m Up to 12 dS/m with improved varieties
    Planting season October–November November–December
    Harvest March–April March–April
    Yield (rain-fed) 0.4–0.8 t/ha 0.6–1.0 t/ha
    Yield (irrigated) 1.5–2.5 t/ha 2.5–4.0 t/ha
    Primary use Livestock feed, some milling for flatbread Livestock feed, camel fodder

    Recommended varieties:

    • Arabi Aswad (عربي أسود): A six-row hulled barley landrace dominant across Yemen's eastern plateaus. Extremely drought-hardy, early maturing (100–115 days), moderate salinity tolerance. Grain is used for animal feed and traditional porridge (عصيدة).
    • Arabi Abyadh (عربي أبيض): Lighter-grained Yemeni landrace, slightly better grain quality for human consumption, similar hardiness.
    • Gustoe / Clipper derivatives: Introduced two-row varieties trialed in Najran and Asir with Saudi research stations. Higher yield potential under irrigation but require more inputs.
    • Tadmor (تدمر): ICARDA-released variety tested in both Yemen and Saudi Arabia, notable for terminal drought tolerance and moderate salinity resistance.

    Barley in Yemen is often planted on wadi edges where residual moisture from seasonal floods persists, or on degraded terraces too saline for wheat. In Najran, barley is grown as a winter fodder crop on smallholdings, often in rotation with alfalfa or sorghum. Seed rates are typically 80–100 kg/ha broadcast, with minimal or no fertilizer application in Yemeni rain-fed systems.

    • عربي أبيض: سلالة يمنية فاتحة الحبوب، جودة أفضل قليلاً للاستهلاك البشري.
    • أصناف غوستو / كليبر المشتقة: أصناف ثنائية الصفوف مُدخلة جُرّبت في نجران وعسير، إنتاجية أعلى تحت الري لكنها تحتاج مدخلات أكثر.

    Q3: How does terraced wheat farming work in the Yemen mountains, and what are its agronomic advantages and limitations?¶

    EN: Yemen's mountain terraces (المدرجات الزراعية) are among the oldest continuous agricultural engineering systems in the world, some dating back over 3,000 years. They are concentrated in the western escarpment (Haraz, Manakhah, Bura'a), the central highlands (Dhamar, Ibb, Taiz highlands), and parts of the Sana'a basin rim.

    Terrace Structure and Function:

    Component Description
    Retaining walls (الجدران) Dry-stone walls 0.5–3 m high, built from local basalt or limestone
    Planting bed (المزرعة) Level soil platform 2–10 m wide behind the wall
    Spillway channels (المفارج) Stone-lined overflow channels directing excess runoff between terraces
    Water intake bunds (السواقي) Small diversion channels capturing hillside runoff
    Soil depth 30–80 cm of accumulated alluvial and aeolian soil

    Agronomic Advantages:

    1. Water harvesting: Terraces capture and hold rainfall that would otherwise be lost as runoff. Effective rainfall utilization increases from 15–25% on unterraced slopes to 60–80% on maintained terraces.
    2. Erosion control: On slopes of 30–60%, terraces reduce soil loss from 50+ t/ha/year to under 5 t/ha/year.
    3. Microclimate moderation: Stone walls absorb heat during the day and release it at night, reducing frost risk by 2–4°C in highland wheat fields.
    4. Extended growing season: Retained moisture allows crops to survive dry spells of 3–4 weeks during the growing season.
    5. Biodiversity: Terraces support polyculture — wheat is often intercropped with lentils, fenugreek (الحلبة), or qat on adjacent terraces.

    Limitations:

    1. Labor intensity: Building and maintaining terraces requires 200–400 person-days/ha. With rural-to-urban migration and conflict displacement, labor shortages cause terrace collapse at an estimated rate of 3–5% per year in affected areas.
    2. Mechanization barrier: Terraces are too narrow and steep for conventional machinery. All tillage, planting, and harvesting is manual or animal-powered (oxen, donkeys).
    3. Low yields: Yields on terraces average 0.6–1.2 t/ha for wheat due to thin soils, limited fertility inputs, and rainfall variability.
    4. Wall collapse: Neglected walls fail during heavy rains, causing cascading erosion of multiple terrace levels. Repair costs are 500–1,500 USD per terrace unit.
    5. Competition with qat: Farmers increasingly convert wheat terraces to qat (Catha edulis), which is more profitable but non-food, reducing grain self-sufficiency.

    1. حصاد المياه: تلتقط المدرجات الأمطار وتحتفظ بها. تزداد كفاءة استخدام الأمطار من ١٥–٢٥% على المنحدرات غير المدرّجة إلى ٦٠–٨٠% على مدرجات مصانة.
    2. مكافحة التعرية: على منحدرات ٣٠–٦٠%، تقلل المدرجات فقدان التربة من أكثر من ٥٠ طن/هـ/سنة إلى أقل من ٥ طن/هـ/سنة.
    3. تعديل المناخ المحلي: تمتص الجدران الحجرية الحرارة نهاراً وتطلقها ليلاً، مما يقلل خطر الصقيع بمقدار ٢–٤ درجات مئوية.
    4. تمديد موسم النمو: تسمح الرطوبة المحتجزة للمحاصيل بتجاوز فترات جفاف ٣–٤ أسابيع.
    5. التنوع البيولوجي: تدعم المدرجات الزراعة المختلطة — يُزرع القمح مع العدس والحلبة أو القات على مدرجات مجاورة.

    القيود:

    1. كثافة العمالة: يتطلب بناء المدرجات وصيانتها ٢٠٠–٤٠٠ يوم عمل/هـ. مع الهجرة والنزوح، تنهار المدرجات بمعدل ٣–٥% سنوياً في المناطق المتأثرة.
    2. عائق المكننة: المدرجات ضيقة وشديدة الانحدار للآلات التقليدية. كل العمليات يدوية أو حيوانية.
    3. إنتاجية منخفضة: متوسط ٠.٦–١.٢ طن/هـ للقمح بسبب رقة التربة وقلة الأسمدة وتذبذب الأمطار.
    4. انهيار الجدران: الجدران المهملة تنهار أثناء الأمطار الغزيرة مسببة تعرية متتالية. تكلفة الإصلاح ٥٠٠–١٥٠٠ دولار لكل وحدة مدرج.
    5. منافسة القات: يحوّل المزارعون مدرجات القمح بشكل متزايد إلى القات الأكثر ربحية لكنه غير غذائي، مما يقلل الاكتفاء الذاتي من الحبوب.

    Q4: How is spate irrigation (السيول) used for wheat and barley production in Yemen's Tihama plain and wadi systems?¶

    EN: Spate irrigation (الري بالسيول) is the traditional flood-based irrigation system practiced in Yemen's coastal Tihama plain and major wadi systems (Wadi Zabid, Wadi Tuban, Wadi Mawr, Wadi Siham, Wadi Hadramawt). It is one of the oldest irrigation methods in the Arabian Peninsula and remains critical for grain production in areas without permanent water sources.

    How Spate Irrigation Works:

    1. Seasonal floods: Monsoon rains in the highlands (July–September) generate flash floods that flow down wadis toward the Tihama lowlands.
    2. Diversion structures (العقم/الصدود): Earthen or stone deflectors built across wadi channels guide floodwater onto agricultural fields. Traditional structures are rebuilt after each major flood season.
    3. Field inundation: Fields (called مساكب or مشارب) are flooded to a depth of 15–40 cm. Water infiltrates into the soil profile, saturating it to a depth of 1.0–1.5 m.
    4. Recession planting: After floodwaters recede (2–7 days), farmers plant wheat or barley directly into the saturated soil. No further irrigation is applied — the crop relies entirely on stored soil moisture.
    5. Sequential field use: Upstream fields receive water first; downstream fields receive later floods. A typical wadi command area has 3–5 distribution levels.

    Spate-Irrigated Wheat and Barley Parameters:

    Parameter Wheat Barley
    Planting time September–November (post-flood) September–October
    Varieties Local landraces (short season, 100–120 days) Arabi Aswad, Arabi Abyadh
    Seed rate 80–120 kg/ha broadcast 70–100 kg/ha broadcast
    Soil moisture at planting Field capacity to 1.0+ m depth Field capacity
    Yield (typical) 0.5–1.5 t/ha 0.4–1.0 t/ha
    Fertilizer Rarely applied None
    Area per flood event 50–500 ha per wadi system Often mixed with wheat
    Key wadis for wheat Wadi Zabid, Wadi Tuban, Wadi Rima' Wadi Mawr, Wadi Hadramawt

    Challenges of Spate Irrigation:

    • Unpredictability: Flood timing, volume, and duration vary enormously between years. A wadi may receive 2–6 flood events per season, or none at all in drought years.
    • Infrastructure damage: Diversion structures are frequently destroyed by large floods. Communities spend 20–40% of agricultural labor on reconstruction.
    • Upstream dam competition: Modern dams (such as Marib Dam and smaller check dams) reduce downstream flood volumes, shrinking spate-irrigated areas.
    • Sedimentation: Floodwaters carry heavy sediment loads (5–15% by volume). While this replenishes soil fertility, it can also bury crops or clog channels.
    • Water rights disputes: Traditional water allocation (النوبة) systems govern distribution order, but conflicts arise during low-flow years.

    AR: الري بالسيول هو نظام الري التقليدي القائم على الفيضانات المُمارَس في سهل تهامة الساحلي ومنظومات الأودية الرئيسية (وادي زبيد، وادي تُبَن، وادي مور، وادي سهام، وادي حضرموت). يُعد من أقدم طرق الري في شبه الجزيرة العربية ويبقى حيوياً لإنتاج الحبوب في مناطق بلا مصادر مائية دائمة.

    آلية عمل الري بالسيول:

    1. الفيضانات الموسمية: تولّد أمطار الرياح الموسمية في المرتفعات (يوليو–سبتمبر) سيولاً جارفة تتدفق عبر الأودية نحو سهول تهامة.
    2. منشآت التحويل (العقم/الصدود): حواجز ترابية أو حجرية تُبنى عبر مجاري الأودية لتوجيه مياه السيول إلى الحقول. تُعاد المنشآت التقليدية بعد كل موسم سيول كبير.
    3. إغمار الحقول: تُغمر الحقول (المساكب أو المشارب) بعمق ١٥–٤٠ سم. تتسرب المياه في التربة لعمق ١.٠–١.٥ م.
    4. الزراعة بعد انحسار المياه: بعد انحسار السيول (٢–٧ أيام)، يزرع المزارعون القمح أو الشعير مباشرة في التربة المشبعة. لا يُطبّق ري إضافي — يعتمد المحصول كلياً على الرطوبة المخزنة.
    5. الاستخدام التتابعي للحقول: تستقبل الحقول العليا المياه أولاً؛ الحقول السفلى تستقبل السيول اللاحقة. منطقة الوادي النموذجية بها ٣–٥ مستويات توزيع.

    • عدم القابلية للتنبؤ: يتباين توقيت وحجم ومدة السيول بشكل كبير بين السنوات.
    • تضرر البنية التحتية: تتدمر منشآت التحويل بالسيول الكبيرة. تُنفق المجتمعات ٢٠–٤٠% من العمل الزراعي على إعادة البناء.
    • منافسة السدود العلوية: تقلل السدود الحديثة من أحجام السيول في المصب، مما يقلّص المساحات المروية بالسيول.
    • الترسيب: تحمل مياه السيول حمولة رسوبية كثيفة (٥–١٥% حجمياً).
    • نزاعات حقوق المياه: تحكم أنظمة النوبة التقليدية ترتيب التوزيع، لكن النزاعات تنشأ في سنوات الجريان المنخفض.

    Q5: What are the traditional grain storage methods (المطامير) used in Yemen, and how effective are they for wheat and barley preservation?¶

    EN: Traditional Yemeni grain storage is a sophisticated indigenous knowledge system developed over millennia to preserve wheat, barley, sorghum, and millet in a region with limited access to modern storage infrastructure. The primary methods include:

    1. Underground Pits (المطامير / Al-Matamir)

    The matmoura (singular: مطمورة) is a bottle-shaped underground pit dug into dry, compact soil or rock:

    Feature Description
    Shape Flask-shaped: narrow neck (40–60 cm), wider chamber (1.0–2.0 m diameter)
    Depth 1.5–3.0 m
    Capacity 500–2,000 kg of grain
    Lining Walls plastered with a mix of clay, straw, and sometimes animal dung; some lined with flat stones
    Sealing Neck sealed with a stone slab and plastered over with mud; location sometimes concealed
    Storage duration 1–5 years (some reports of 7+ years for wheat)
    Temperature Stable 18–22°C year-round underground
    Humidity Very low in arid highland soils (< 30% RH in the chamber)

    Effectiveness: When properly constructed in dry highland soils (Dhamar, Sana'a, Al-Bayda), matamir can preserve wheat and barley with grain losses under 5% for 2–3 years. The low-oxygen, cool, dry environment inhibits insect reproduction and fungal growth. Grain moisture must be below 12% at storage. Losses increase significantly (15–30%) if pits are dug in poorly drained soils or if grain is stored above 14% moisture.

    2. Elevated Stone Granaries (المخازن الحجرية)

    Common in mountain villages, these are small stone buildings (2–4 m tall) with thick walls, raised floors for ventilation, and small sealed openings. They protect against rodents and moisture. Capacity: 1–5 tonnes. Still in active use in Haraz, Manakhah, and parts of Ibb.

    3. Sealed Pottery Vessels (الخوابي)

    Large clay jars (50–200 kg capacity) sealed with beeswax or clay. Used for smaller quantities and valued seed stocks. Provide excellent hermetic storage for 1–2 seasons.

    4. Woven Containers (القفف/الأكياس)

    Woven palm or grass containers, sometimes lined with animal skin. Less effective — grain losses of 10–20% within one season due to insect and moisture penetration. Used mainly for short-term storage (1–3 months).

    Preservation Practices:

    • Grain is thoroughly sun-dried on rooftops (2–3 days) before storage to achieve < 12% moisture
    • Some farmers add dried neem leaves (النيم), ash, or dried hot pepper as natural insect repellents
    • Pits are cleaned and sometimes fumigated with smoke before refilling
    • Highland farmers traditionally store enough grain for 1–2 years as a food security buffer

    Modern Challenges:

    Traditional storage is declining due to conflict-driven displacement, loss of indigenous knowledge transfer between generations, availability of plastic sacks (which are less effective but more convenient), and urbanization. International agencies have introduced hermetic storage bags (PICS bags) as a modern adaptation of the matmoura principle with good results.

    Q6: What are the major challenges facing wheat and barley production in Yemen and southern Saudi Arabia, including declining output, water scarcity, and conflict impacts?¶

    EN: Wheat and barley production in Yemen and southern Saudi Arabia faces a convergence of structural, environmental, and geopolitical challenges that have driven a severe and ongoing decline in output. Yemen, which once exported grain, now imports over 90% of its wheat.

    1. Declining Production Trends:

    Indicator Yemen Southern Saudi Arabia (Asir, Najran, Jazan)
    Wheat area (peak vs. current) ~150,000 ha (1990s) → ~45,000 ha (est. 2025) Part of national phase-down; localized cultivation persists
    Wheat production ~180,000 tonnes (1990s) → ~50,000 tonnes (est. 2025) Shifted from export to local/regional consumption only
    Barley area ~70,000 ha (1990s) → ~20,000 ha (est. 2025) Small-scale, primarily fodder
    National yield average 0.8–1.2 t/ha (rain-fed), 2.0 t/ha (irrigated) 3.5–5.0 t/ha (irrigated, but area declining)
    Self-sufficiency (wheat) < 5% (down from ~25% in 1990s) Saudi Arabia phased out wheat self-sufficiency program

    2. Water Scarcity:

    • Groundwater depletion: Yemen's water table is dropping 2–6 m/year in major basins (Sana'a basin projected dry by 2030s). Unregulated tube wells (estimated 70,000+) pump aquifers far beyond recharge.
    • Qat competition: Qat consumes an estimated 40% of Yemen's irrigation water despite being a non-food crop. Farmers choose qat over wheat because it generates 5–10x more income per hectare.
    • Saudi water policy: Saudi Arabia deliberately reduced wheat production starting in 2008 to conserve fossil aquifer reserves (Saq-Ram aquifer). Southern regions shifted toward less water-intensive crops.
    • Climate change: Rainfall variability has increased. The highlands experience more erratic monsoon patterns, and Tihama spate flows have become less predictable.

    3. Conflict Impacts (Yemen):

    Impact Area Description
    Agricultural infrastructure Destruction of irrigation systems, terraces, roads, and storage facilities. An estimated 30–40% of irrigation infrastructure damaged since 2015.
    Input supply chains Fertilizer imports disrupted; prices increased 200–400%. Fuel for pumps scarce and expensive. Certified seed distribution collapsed.
    Labor displacement 4+ million internally displaced; rural labor force depleted. Terrace maintenance abandoned in conflict zones.
    Land mines Agricultural land contaminated with mines and unexploded ordnance, particularly in Marib, Al-Jawf, Hodeidah, and Taiz.
    Market disruption Road closures and port blockades disrupt grain markets. Price of flour increased 150–300% in some areas.
    Research collapse Agricultural Research and Extension Authority (AREA) centers in Dhamar and Taiz damaged or non-functional. Variety development and extension services halted.
    Food aid dependency Imported wheat food aid depresses local wheat prices, reducing farmer incentive to plant.

    4. Structural and Economic Challenges:

    • Lack of investment: Government agricultural spending in Yemen was minimal even before the conflict. Extension services are virtually non-existent in most areas.
    • Fragmented landholdings: Average farm size is 0.5–2 ha, making mechanization and economies of scale impossible.
    • Seed system breakdown: No functioning certified seed multiplication system in Yemen. Farmers recycle seed for 5–10+ generations, leading to genetic deterioration and disease susceptibility.
    • Climate adaptation gap: Improved drought-tolerant and heat-tolerant varieties exist (from ICARDA, CIMMYT) but cannot be distributed or demonstrated at scale.
    • Post-harvest losses: Estimated at 15–25% due to inadequate threshing, drying, and storage — especially where traditional methods have been abandoned but not replaced.

    5. Potential Interventions:

    Intervention Feasibility Expected Impact
    Terrace rehabilitation programs Medium (labor + funding intensive) Restore 30–50% of abandoned wheat area in highlands
    Hermetic storage bag distribution High (low cost, proven technology) Reduce post-harvest losses from 20% to < 5%
    Community seed banks Medium (requires institutional support) Preserve landraces, improve seed quality
    Spate irrigation modernization Medium (engineering + community buy-in) Increase irrigated area by 20–30% per wadi system
    Solar-powered micro-irrigation High in southern Saudi, medium in Yemen Reduce fuel dependency, extend growing season
    ICARDA/CIMMYT variety introduction High (varieties exist, need delivery mechanism) Potential yield increase of 30–50% over local landraces

    Q7: What role does the Islamic agricultural calendar play in timing wheat and barley planting in Yemen and southern Saudi Arabia?¶

    EN: Traditional Yemeni and southern Saudi farmers use a sophisticated indigenous calendrical system that integrates Islamic lunar months, star-based seasons (الأنواء), and solar agricultural markers to determine optimal planting, irrigation, and harvest timing for wheat and barley. This system predates modern meteorology and remains in active use, particularly among older farmers and in areas without extension services.

    Star-Based Seasonal Markers (منازل القمر / الأنواء):

    The 28 lunar mansions (manazil) divide the year into periods associated with specific weather patterns and agricultural activities:

    Star Period (النوء) Approximate Gregorian Dates Agricultural Significance for Wheat/Barley
    سهيل (Suhail / Canopus rising) Mid-August Signal to prepare highland fields for planting; end of extreme summer heat
    الوسم (Al-Wasm) October–November Primary wheat planting window in highlands; first autumn rains expected
    المربعانية (Al-Murba'aniyya) December 7 – January 15 (40 days) Coldest period; wheat in tillering stage. Frost risk in highlands above 2,500 m. No planting during this period
    الشبط (Al-Shabat) Late January – February End of coldest period; wheat enters stem elongation. Barley begins heading in lower areas
    سعد الذابح / سعد بلع / سعد السعود (Sa'd series) February – March Warming period; wheat heading and flowering. Critical irrigation timing if available
    الحميم (Al-Hamim) June – July Harvest completion for highland wheat before monsoon rains begin

    Local Proverbs and Sayings:

    • "إذا طلع سهيل، بَرَد الليل" — When Suhail rises, nights cool (signal to prepare for planting)
    • "الوسم ربيع الزرع" — Al-Wasm is the spring of planting

    Q3: How does terraced wheat farming work in the Yemen mountains, and why is this system considered globally significant?¶

    EN: Yemen's mountain terraces (المدرجات الزراعية) represent one of the oldest continuously farmed landscapes on Earth, dating back over 3,000 years. They are concentrated in the western escarpment from Sa'dah in the north to Taiz in the south, at elevations of 1,500–3,000 m.

    Terrace architecture and function:

    Component Description Agricultural Purpose
    Stone walls (الجدران الحجرية) Dry-stone retaining walls 0.5–3 m high Prevent soil erosion on slopes of 30–60°
    Flat planting surface (السطح الزراعي) Level beds 2–10 m wide Allow water infiltration, root development
    Drainage channels (المسارب) Stone-lined channels between terraces Controlled runoff distribution during rains
    Sediment traps (المصائد) Depressions at terrace edges Capture nutrient-rich eroded material
    Water diversion (السواقي) Channels from springs or rain catchment Supplemental irrigation for critical growth stages

    Wheat cultivation cycle on terraces:

    1. Land preparation (September): Walls repaired after summer rains; soil turned with traditional ox-drawn plow (المحراث) or hand hoe
    2. Sowing (October–November): Local wheat broadcast-sown at 80–120 kg/ha; some farmers mix wheat and barley for risk reduction
    3. Maintenance (December–February): Minimal inputs; occasional hand-weeding; rely on residual moisture and winter rains
    4. Harvest (March–May): Hand-sickle harvest; threshing by animal trampling on circular threshing floors (البيادر); winnowing by wind

    Global significance: - UNESCO has recognized Yemeni terraces as a candidate for World Heritage status - They conserve thin mountain soils that would otherwise erode completely - They recharge groundwater by slowing runoff - They preserve unique wheat landraces with drought and cold tolerance genes valuable for global breeding programs - Annual terrace maintenance requires 40–60 person-days per hectare, sustaining rural livelihoods

    Threats: Terrace abandonment has accelerated since the 1970s due to urban migration, qat cultivation displacing wheat, labor shortages, and conflict. An estimated 30–40% of highland terraces are now degraded or abandoned.

    Q4: How does spate irrigation (ري السيول) work for wheat and barley in Yemen's Tihama plain and wadi systems, and how does it compare to conventional irrigation?¶

    EN: Spate irrigation is one of the oldest water harvesting techniques in the world, practiced for millennia in Yemen's western lowlands (Tihama) and wadi systems. It captures seasonal flood flows from mountain catchments and diverts them onto cultivated fields.

    How spate irrigation works:

    1. Catchment rainfall (July–September): Summer monsoon rains fall on the western highlands (500–1,000 mm)
    2. Flood generation: Runoff concentrates in wadis (seasonal riverbeds) producing flash floods
    3. Diversion structures (الموجِّه / العقم): Earth-and-stone bunds or concrete weirs deflect floodwater into canal systems
    4. Field flooding: Water is spread across leveled fields in sequence; upstream fields receive water first, then overflow to downstream plots
    5. Soil saturation: Fields absorb 150–300 mm of water per flood event; fine sediment deposits add 2–5 mm of nutrient-rich alluvium annually
    6. Planting: Wheat or barley sown directly into saturated soil after floodwater recedes (October–November)
    7. Crop growth: Plants rely entirely on stored soil moisture; no further irrigation

    Key wadi systems for spate-irrigated grain:

    Wadi System Location Area (ha) Primary Crops Annual Floods
    Wadi Zabid Tihama (Hodeidah) 18,000 Wheat, sorghum, millet 3–6 events
    Wadi Mawr Tihama (Hajjah) 12,000 Barley, wheat, sesame 2–5 events
    Wadi Siham Tihama (Hodeidah) 8,000 Sorghum, barley 3–5 events
    Wadi Tuban Lahj/Aden 6,000 Wheat, vegetables 2–4 events
    Wadi Bana Abyan 5,000 Wheat, cotton 2–4 events

    Q5: What are المطامير (underground grain silos), and how are they used for traditional wheat and barley storage in Yemen?¶

    EN: المطامير (al-matameer, singular: مطمورة / matmoura) are traditional underground grain storage pits that have been used in Yemen and the broader Arabian Peninsula for centuries. They represent an ingenious low-cost, zero-energy solution for long-term cereal storage in hot, arid climates.

    Construction and design:

    Feature Description
    Shape Flask or bell-shaped pit, narrow mouth (40–60 cm), wider base (1.5–2.5 m)
    Depth 1.5–3.0 m below ground
    Capacity 0.5–3.0 tonnes of grain per pit
    Lining Plastered with a mixture of clay, ash, and animal dung; sometimes lined with straw
    Cover Sealed with flat stone slab, covered with earth and dung plaster
    Location Elevated, well-drained ground; often inside or adjacent to the home compound
    Ventilation Minimal (anaerobic environment is intentional)

    How المطامير preserve grain:

    1. Anaerobic atmosphere: The sealed pit depletes oxygen as grain respires, creating a CO2-rich environment (up to 15–20% CO2) that kills insect pests and inhibits fungal growth
    2. Temperature stability: Underground temperature remains 20–25 degrees C even when surface temperatures exceed 40 degrees C, slowing biological activity
    3. Low humidity: The clay/ash lining absorbs excess moisture; grain must be dried to less than 12% moisture content before storage
    4. Pest exclusion: The sealed stone cover and earth layer prevent rodent and insect entry

    Storage duration and grain quality:

    Storage Period Grain Condition Notes
    1–2 years Excellent Full viability for seed and consumption
    3–5 years Good Slight flavor change, still edible
    5–10 years Adequate Reduced germination, edible after processing
    10+ years Emergency use Documented cases of 15-year storage in Yemen highlands

    Cultural and strategic significance: - المطامير serve as a household and community food security reserve, critical during droughts, sieges, and conflict - During the current Yemen conflict, families with functioning matameer have been significantly more food-secure - The knowledge of construction and management is passed through generations but is declining among younger farmers - Some communities maintain communal matameer managed by village leaders

    • يحتاج القات رياً على مدار العام، مما يسرّع استنزاف الخزانات الجوفية
    • عجزت الحكومة عن تنظيم توسع القات بسبب القيود السياسية

    التحدي ٤: التغير المناخي

    • ارتفاع درجات الحرارة (+١٫٢ درجة مئوية في مرتفعات اليمن منذ ١٩٦٠)
    • أنماط أمطار أكثر تذبذباً مع فترات جفاف أطول
    • تزايد وتيرة أحداث الفيضانات الشديدة التي تُتلف بدل أن تروي
    • انتقال نطاقات زراعة القمح الصالحة إلى ارتفاعات أعلى
    • ضغوط آفات وأمراض جديدة (مثل انتقال صدأ القمح إلى مناطق لم تتأثر سابقاً)

    التحدي ٥: العوامل الاقتصادية والهيكلية

    • دقيق القمح المستورد أرخص من الحبوب المنتجة محلياً
    • غياب الميكنة على الأراضي المدرّجة (أكثر من ٩٠٪ عمالة يدوية)
    • هجرة الشباب إلى المدن مما يقلل توفّر العمالة الزراعية
    • فقدان المعرفة التقليدية (صيانة المدرجات، إدارة السيول، بناء المطامير)
    • خدمات إرشاد حكومية ودعم مدخلات في حدها الأدنى
    • غياب التأمين على المحاصيل أو آليات دعم الأسعار

    ري بالطاقة الشمسية (بديل الديزل) تبنٍّ متزايد عالي - خفض تكلفة الطاقة ٦٠–٨٠٪

    Q7: What traditional and modern grain processing methods are used for wheat and barley in Yemen, and what local food products do they produce?¶

    EN: Wheat and barley hold deep cultural significance in Yemeni and southern Saudi cuisine. Traditional processing methods persist alongside limited modern milling, particularly in rural areas where access to commercial flour is restricted by conflict and logistics.

    Traditional processing methods:

    Method Arabic Name Description Products
    Stone hand-mill الرحى (ar-raha) Two circular stones (30–50 cm diameter) turned by hand; women's domain Whole wheat flour, cracked grain
    Water mill الطاحون المائي Stone mill powered by water channels on mountain terraces; communal facility Fine flour, medium grind
    Animal-driven mill الطاحون الحيواني Larger stones turned by donkey or camel walking in circles Bulk flour for villages
    Pounding mortar المهباش / الجرن Large stone mortar and wooden pestle for hulling barley and cracking grain Hulled barley, cracked wheat (جريش)
    Roasting/parching الحميسة / القلي Grain roasted in flat iron pan over wood fire before grinding Roasted flour (سويق), enhanced flavor and shelf life
    Sun drying التجفيف الشمسي Harvested grain spread on flat rooftops or threshing floors for 3–7 days Dried grain (less than 12% moisture) for storage

    Q8: What role can technology and platforms like SAHOOL play in reviving wheat and barley cultivation in Yemen and southern Saudi Arabia?¶

    EN: Digital agricultural platforms have significant potential to support grain cultivation revival in this region, provided they are designed for the specific constraints of low-connectivity, conflict-affected, and resource-scarce environments.

    Key technology interventions and SAHOOL platform alignment:

    Challenge Technology Solution Platform Capability
    Loss of traditional knowledge Digital documentation of terrace management, spate irrigation, matameer construction Farm documentation skill, knowledge base with 91 agricultural documents
    Variety selection uncertainty AI-powered variety recommendation based on elevation, soil, rainfall Crop advisory skill, agricultural AI models registry (50+ models)

    Conflict-related data loss Offline-first architecture with encrypted local storage Mobile sync with conflict resolution, Drift database with SQLCipher encryption

    Offline-first design is critical: In Yemen, internet connectivity is available to less than 30% of the rural population, and power outages are frequent. SAHOOL's offline-first architecture with background sync and local AI inference (Ollama, edge devices) directly addresses this constraint.

    Specific recommendations for grain cultivation support:

    1. Highland terrace module: Map existing terraces using satellite imagery, track maintenance status, alert when degradation is detected, connect to rehabilitation programs
    2. Spate irrigation forecasting: Integrate upstream rainfall data with wadi flood models to give farmers 6–24 hours advance notice of spate flows
    3. Landrace seed bank registry: Digital catalog of local wheat and barley varieties with geo-tagged collection sites, agronomic characteristics, and farmer custodian contacts
    4. Matameer monitoring: Simple IoT sensors (temperature, humidity) placed in traditional storage pits to alert farmers if conditions deteriorate
    5. Cooperative grain aggregation: Enable smallholders to collectively market grain, reducing per-unit transport costs and improving bargaining power
    6. Conflict-resilient data: Encrypted, distributed storage of farm records that survives infrastructure disruption

    2. تنبؤ ري السيول: دمج بيانات الأمطار من أعالي الأودية مع نماذج فيضانات الأودية لإعطاء المزارعين إشعاراً مسبقاً بـ ٦–٢٤ ساعة بتدفقات السيول
    3. سجل بنك بذور الأصناف المحلية: كتالوج رقمي لأصناف القمح والشعير المحلية مع مواقع الجمع المعلّمة جغرافياً والخصائص الزراعية وبيانات المزارعين الحافظين
    4. مراقبة المطامير: مستشعرات إنترنت أشياء بسيطة (حرارة، رطوبة) تُوضع في حفر التخزين التقليدية لتنبيه المزارعين إذا تدهورت الظروف
    5. تجميع الحبوب التعاوني: تمكين صغار المزارعين من تسويق الحبوب جماعياً، تقليل تكاليف النقل لكل وحدة وتحسين القوة التفاوضية
    6. بيانات مقاومة للنزاعات: تخزين مشفّر وموزّع لسجلات المزارع يصمد أمام تعطّل البنية التحتية
